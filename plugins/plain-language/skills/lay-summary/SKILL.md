# Plain-language versions of a paper

Write for an intelligent reader who is not in the field. Accuracy comes first: a plain version that overstates the finding is worse than none.

## 1. Pin down the facts first

From the paper, write down before drafting:

- the question, in one everyday sentence;
- who or what was studied, and how many;
- the main finding, with its size in plain numbers ("about 1 in 5", "twice as likely", "12% lower");
- how certain it is, and the main limitation;
- what it does **not** show (correlation is not cause; mice are not people; a pilot is not proof).

Use only what the paper says. If a number you need is not in it, leave it out rather than estimate it.

## 2. Pick the form

| Form | Reader | Length | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lay summary | public, participants, funders | 150–250 words, or the journal's limit | Often required by journals and funders; follow their headings if given. |
| Press release | journalists | 400–600 words | Headline, then the finding in the first paragraph, a quote from the user (draft it, marked for them to approve), then context and limits, then "Notes to editors" with the citation and DOI. |
| Policy brief | officials, practitioners | 1–2 pages | Key message box, the problem, the evidence, what it means, recommendations only as far as the evidence goes. |

If the user did not say which, ask. If they need several, write the lay summary first; the others build on it.

## 3. Write plainly

- Aim for a reading age of about 12–14 (UK) or grade 8 (US). Short sentences, about 15–20 words on average.
- Everyday words: "use" not "utilise", "show" not "demonstrate". Define any term you must keep in a few words the first time.
- No abbreviations unless the reader knows them (DNA, UK); spell the rest out.
- Active voice, and say who did it: "We asked 300 nurses …".
- Numbers as natural frequencies or simple comparisons, not odds ratios or p-values. Say "about" when rounding.
- Lead with why it matters to the reader, then what was found.

## 4. Stay accurate

- Keep the hedges the evidence needs: "linked to", "may", "in this group", "early results".
- Never turn an association into a cause, a lab or animal result into a human one, or a small study into a settled fact.
- Include the main limitation in plain words.
- Do not add advice the paper does not support ("people should eat more …").

## 5. Check before handing over

Re-read against the facts from step 1: every number matches the paper, nothing is claimed that the paper does not claim, and the limitation is there. Give the word count and say which form and reading level you aimed for. Mark any quote you drafted for the user as needing their approval.
