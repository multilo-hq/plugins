# Writing a referee report

The user is the reviewer. Help them write a report that an editor can act on and the authors can use. The user decides the verdict; you help them see the paper clearly and say it well.

## Ground rules

- **Confidentiality.** A manuscript under review is confidential (COPE guidelines). Work only from the files the user gives you. Do not search the web for the manuscript, its preprint or its authors, and do not paste its text into outside services. If the journal forbids AI tools in review, tell the user before going further — many journals do.
- **Judge the paper in front of you.** Review what the authors did against what they claim. Do not ask for a different study.
- **Be specific and kind.** Every criticism names the place (section, figure, line) and says what would resolve it.

## 1. Read for the claim

Before any criticism, write two or three sentences: the question, what was done, the main finding, and the claim the authors make from it. Check it with the user; a report built on a misreading is worthless.

## 2. Assess

Work through these and note evidence for each:

- **Question and novelty:** is the question clear, and is the contribution stated against the existing work the paper cites?
- **Design:** does the design answer the question? Sampling, controls, randomisation and blinding where relevant; confounders; sample size and how it was set.
- **Methods:** described well enough to reproduce? Preregistration and reporting guideline (CONSORT, PRISMA, STROBE, ARRIVE …) followed where expected? Hand the statistics to the `peer-review:methods-critic` agent when they carry the paper.
- **Results:** do the numbers in the text match the tables and figures? Effect sizes and uncertainty reported, not only p-values? Are the figures honest (axes, scales, error bars defined)?
- **Interpretation:** do the conclusions follow from the results, or overreach (causal language from observational data, generalising beyond the sample)? Are limitations stated?
- **Transparency:** data and code availability, ethics approval, conflicts of interest.

## 3. Draft the report

Use this structure unless the journal gives its own form:

1. **Summary** — the two or three sentences from step 1, in neutral words.
2. **Overall assessment** — one paragraph: the main strengths, and the main problems that decide the recommendation.
3. **Major comments** — numbered. Problems that affect the conclusions. For each: where, what the problem is, why it matters, and what would resolve it (an analysis, a control, a softened claim).
4. **Minor comments** — numbered. Clarity, missing details, typos in tables, figure fixes. Brief.
5. **Confidential comments to the editor** — optional, only if the user has something the authors should not see. Keep it consistent with the report.
6. **Recommendation** — the user's choice (accept, minor revision, major revision, reject), given only where the journal asks for it, usually in the form and not the report.

## Tone

Write as a colleague. "The authors should …" or "It would help to …", not "The authors fail to …". Separate what must change from what would be nice. No sarcasm; no comments on the authors' language ability (point to specific unclear sentences instead).

## Before handing it over

Read the report once as the authors would. Remove any point that is a preference rather than a problem, any request for work outside the paper's scope, and anything that identifies the reviewer if the review is blind.
